\large what makes a small machine tractable, and where the line falls}

\date{August 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Small, fully explicit machines can encode open problems in number theory.
This paper is about the boundary between those that do and those that
merely look as though they do. We work in FRACTRAN, Conway's minimal
universal language, on the live $\mathrm{BBf}(23)$ Busy Beaver holdout
list. We isolate the property that separates tractable machines from the
rest --- we call it \emph{rigidity} --- turn it into a certificate class
with a decidable checking problem, prove the checker sound, formalize
that soundness proof in Lean~4, and then \emph{search} for certificates
automatically. The result is an end-to-end decision procedure that decides
$25$ of the $694$ machines on the refined holdout list, sixteen of them
not previously decided by anyone. Nine of the $25$ additionally carry
individual, \texttt{sorry}-free Lean proofs.

We have tried to write the paper we would have wanted before starting.
The mathematics involved is elementary; what is not obvious is
\emph{which} elementary facts matter, and why the method stops exactly
where it does. Three lessons generalize beyond FRACTRAN. First, priority
is what makes these machines universal, and closed-form orbits are what
make individual ones tractable --- rigidity is the decidable margin of a
holdout list, not an accident of which machines were studied. Second,
guard checking over a parametric family reduces to two facts of
independent interest: an extreme-point principle for jointly affine
functions, and the decidability of eventual positivity in a small
expression class that sits just inside the frontier of the Positivity
problem. Third --- and this one cost us a wrong proof --- verifying that
a competing rule is \emph{disabled} across a run cannot be done by
testing its guard's negation at the endpoints; one conjunct must be
pinned. We give a machine-checked counterexample.

We also report a negative result that saves future effort: the holdout
list is already canonical under the only prime-relabelling symmetry that
transfers the halting question, so no machines can be removed by
relabelling alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A FRACTRAN program is a finite ordered list of positive rationals. The
state is a positive integer $n$; a step replaces $n$ by $n \cdot p/q$ for
the \emph{first} listed fraction $p/q$ with $q \mid n$; the program halts
when none applies. That is the entire language, and it is
Turing-complete~\cite{conway1987}.

The Busy Beaver variant asks, among all programs of description size at
most $k$, for the longest finite run. As with Turing machines, the
difficulty is not finding long runners but \emph{certifying} the
non-halters: a single program that runs forever must be proved to do so.
$\mathrm{BBf}(23)$ is the current frontier, with $21{,}233$ machines
officially undecided as of 10 July 2026 and a refined list of
$694$~\cite{bbfractran}.

The reason a specific tiny program can resist proof is worth stating
plainly, because it is the subject of this paper. Some machines have
orbits with closed forms; you can write down the state after the $i$-th
phase as a formula in $i$. Others choose their next move from arithmetic
detail --- a digit, a residue, a valuation --- that changes
unpredictably as the state grows. For the first kind, non-halting is a
finite algebraic identity. For the second, it is a Collatz-type problem,
and Collatz-type problems in this generality are
undecidable~\cite{conway1972,kurtzsimon}.

\paragraph{What a reader should take away.} We have organized the paper
around ideas rather than around our machines.

\begin{itemize}
\item \S\ref{sec:vas}: FRACTRAN \emph{is} a priority counter machine, and

\item \S\ref{sec:limits}: where the method stops, what a machine looks
  like just past the boundary, and a measured negative result about
  symmetry.
\end{itemize}

\section{FRACTRAN is a priority counter machine}\label{sec:vas}

Factor the state. Writing $n = \prod_j p_j^{\,v_j}$, the divisibility
test $q \mid n$ becomes a conjunction of lower bounds on the exponents
$v_j$, and multiplying by $p/q$ becomes adding a fixed integer vector.
So a FRACTRAN program is exactly a finite set of guarded vector-addition
rules over $\mathbb{N}^d$, with the guards being lower bounds and the
rules tried in a fixed priority order.

This is not a new observation --- it is how Conway's universality proof
works, and the machine model is Minsky's~\cite{minsky1967} --- but it is
worth dwelling on, because it places FRACTRAN precisely against a
decidability boundary that is one of the most studied in computer
science.

Drop the priority. What remains --- guarded vector addition where any
enabled rule may fire --- is a \emph{vector addition system}, equivalently
a Petri net~\cite{karpmiller}. For those, reachability is
\textbf{decidable}, a celebrated result~\cite{mayr,kosaraju} whose exact
complexity was settled only recently as Ackermann-complete~\cite{ack}.
Add priority --- ``fire the first enabled rule'' --- and the model
becomes Turing-complete, so reachability is undecidable. FRACTRAN's
entire computational power sits in the word \emph{first}.

That reframing pays off immediately. It tells us that any hope of a
general decision procedure is misplaced, so the question becomes which
\emph{fragment} is decidable; and it tells us where to look, because
priority only matters when two rules are simultaneously enabled. A
machine whose orbit keeps the competing guards separated is, in effect,
running a plain vector addition system, and those we can reason about.
The certificates of \S\ref{sec:cert} are, from this angle, exactly
witnesses that the priority never has to be adjudicated in a way that
depends on unbounded arithmetic detail.

\section{Rigidity: the decidable margin}\label{sec:rigid}

Consider the first steps of a typical holdout. A rule fires while some
coordinate lasts, then another takes over; the pattern repeats at a
larger scale; and the question is whether the pattern always repeats.
Two things can happen.

The pattern can be \emph{rigid}: the sequence of rules in each repetition
is the same word, with the repetition counts given by a formula in the
phase index. Then the state at the start of each phase is a formula too,
and non-halting becomes: do these formulas keep the guards positive
forever? That is an algebraic question about finitely many expressions.

Or the pattern can \emph{consume digits}: the rule chosen at some point
depends on the residue of the current state modulo something that grows,
or on the $2$-adic valuation of a quantity that changes at every phase.
Then no formula in the phase index describes the word, and the orbit is
the branching, pseudorandom object familiar from
$3x+1$~\cite{lagarias}. For such maps, the ``expected'' behaviour is
easy to compute and hard to prove --- the state of the art on Collatz
itself is an almost-all theorem~\cite{tao}, and even that says nothing
about any \emph{particular} orbit.

The distinction is not about our ingenuity. It is a property of the
machine, it is visible in a bounded simulation (\S\ref{sec:mine}), and it
predicts which machines have already fallen to earlier work. Our census
of the $\mathrm{BBf}(23)$ list bears this out: rigid machines are a
sizeable minority of the official list and a \emph{tiny} minority of what
survives after the community's deciders have run --- exactly what one
expects if rigidity is the decidable margin being steadily consumed.

\section{Certificates: the proof object}\label{sec:cert}

If a machine is rigid, what exactly should we write down?

The instinct from program verification is to look for an \emph{inductive
invariant}: a property preserved by every step, implying non-halting.
That is not quite the right shape here, because the interesting property
is not preserved step by step --- it is restored once per phase, after
thousands of steps that violate it. The right object is therefore a
\emph{boundary family} together with a proof that it reproduces itself.

\begin{definition}[rigid phase certificate]\label{def:cert}
Let $\mathrm{EXP} = \{\,a + bn + c\,2^{n} : a,b,c \in \mathbb{Q}\,\}$. A
certificate consists of: an \emph{entry} $(E, i_0)$ --- the machine
reaches the $i_0$-th boundary in $E$ steps from its start; a
\emph{boundary family} $B(n)$ with each coordinate in $\mathrm{EXP}$; and,
for each residue class of $n$ modulo $2$, a list of \emph{stages}
describing the phase word: a \emph{run} of one rule repeated an
$\mathrm{EXP}$ number of times, or a \emph{block} --- a fixed short word
repeated an $\mathrm{EXP}$ number of times, whose start state is affine
in the block index.
\end{definition}

Why $\mathrm{EXP}$? Because it is the smallest class closed under what
the phases actually do. A run consumes a coordinate that was built up by
doubling, so counts like $2^{n}-1$ appear; step counts accumulate linear
corrections, so $n$ appears; and the class must be closed under
$n \mapsto n+1$ and under integer combinations, which it is. It is also
small enough that $\{1, n, 2^{n}\}$ are linearly independent over
$\mathbb{Q}$ --- so two states are equal as formulas exactly when their
nine coefficients agree, and \emph{symbolic state equality is a finite
check}. That single fact is what makes the whole scheme work.

\begin{theorem}[soundness]\label{thm:sound}
If the checker accepts a certificate for $M$, then $M$ never halts from
its start. \Lean
\end{theorem}

The proof splits into a dynamical core and a geometric part. The core is
short enough to state in full.

\begin{theorem}[phase induction]\label{thm:A}
Let $B : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}^d$. If the machine reaches $B(i_0)$
from its start, and for every $i \ge i_0$ there is $m \ge 1$ with
$B(i) \xrightarrow{m} B(i+1)$, then it never halts. \Lean
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By induction, for every $k$ the machine completes at least $k$ steps: the
entry is the base case, and each phase contributes at least one more
step. A machine that completes arbitrarily many steps never halts.
\end{proof}

Everything else in the checker exists to establish the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:A} for all $i$ at once.

\paragraph{Relation to Busy Beaver deciders.} The community's deciders for
Turing machines are broadly of two kinds: those that find a regular
invariant of the tape language (closed tape languages, finite-automata
reductions), and those that certify a repeating macro-structure
(translated cyclers, bouncers). Certificates here are of the second kind,
lifted to arithmetic: the ``macro-structure'' is a phase, and the
repetition is witnessed by algebra rather than by tape matching. The
precedent for taking such proofs seriously at scale is
Coq-BB5~\cite{coqbb5}, which settled $\mathrm{BB}(5)$ with a verified
decider plus per-machine certificates --- the architecture we follow.

\section{Three principles that make checking finite}\label{sec:three}

A certificate asserts something about infinitely many phase indices and,
within each phase, about a number of steps that grows exponentially. The
checker must reduce that to finitely much arithmetic. Three facts do it.

\subsection{The extreme-point principle}

Inside a block the state is affine in the block index $k$; within a run it
is affine in the run counter $t$. So every guard value has the shape
$a + bk + ct$ over a rectangle $[0,K] \times [0,L]$.

\begin{theorem}[corner principle]\label{thm:B}
If $a + bk + ct \ge 0$ at the four corners of the rectangle, it is
$\ge 0$ throughout; likewise for $\le \theta$. \Lean
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A linear function on an interval attains its extremes at an endpoint.
Apply this in $k$ along the two extreme rows, then in $t$ for fixed $k$.
\end{proof}

This is the polyhedral fact that linear programs attain optima at
vertices, in its smallest useful instance. It converts ``check every one
of $2^{n}$ steps'' into ``check four states''.

\subsection{Eventual positivity, and a frontier we sit just inside}

The corners are themselves formulas in $\mathrm{EXP}$, so the checker
must decide statements like ``$g(n) \ge 1$ for every $n \ge n_0$ in an
arithmetic progression'' for $g \in \mathrm{EXP}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:evpos}
This is decidable. \Proved
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $g = a + bn + c2^{n}$. If $c < 0$, or $c = 0$ and $b < 0$, then $g$
eventually falls below any bound: answer no. Otherwise the increment of
$g$ along the progression is eventually nonnegative, at an explicitly
computable index $N$; check $n_0 \le n \le N$ directly.
\end{proof}

It is worth knowing how narrow the ledge is. $\mathrm{EXP}$ consists of
linear recurrence sequences of order at most three with the very tame
characteristic roots $1, 1, 2$. Asking whether a general linear recurrence
stays nonnegative is the \emph{Positivity problem}, and it is open in
general; decidability is known only at low order, and even there the
proofs are delicate and connected to hard Diophantine
questions~\cite{ouaknineworrell}. We are not doing anything clever
here --- we are working in a class chosen small enough that positivity is
trivial. That is a design decision, and it is the honest reason the
checker terminates.

\subsection{Disabling a rival: pin one conjunct}

The third principle is the one we got wrong first, and it is the most
transferable.

To justify that a certified rule really fires, one must show that every
higher-priority rule is \emph{disabled} throughout the run. A rule is
disabled when \emph{some} conjunct of its guard fails. The tempting check
is: verify ``some conjunct fails'' at the two ends of the run, and appeal
to the corner principle. This is invalid.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:unsound}
There are affine conjunct values $p+ut$, $q+vt$ and a length $n$ such
that at $t=0$ and at $t=n$ some conjunct fails, yet at an intermediate
$t$ both hold --- so the rival rule fires in the middle of a run the
check accepted. \Lean
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $p+ut = t$ and $q+vt = 2-t$ with $n=2$: at $t=0$ the first vanishes,
at $t=2$ the second does, and at $t=1$ both equal $1$.
\end{proof}

The error is elementary once seen: the corner principle applies to a
single affine function, and ``disabled'' is a \emph{disjunction} of
affine conditions, whose truth set is not convex. The fix is to require
one fixed conjunct to fail at all corners, which is again a single affine
function and so is covered by Theorem~\ref{thm:B}.

We flag this because the same trap is available to any verifier that
reasons about guarded transitions by interval or corner testing, and
because it has an audible signature: the failing certificate is not
detected by more simulation --- the machine behaves exactly as expected
at both ends of every run --- but only by asking which conjunct is doing
the work. In the Lean development the discipline shows up as a rule
typically needing \emph{two} firing lemmas, because different coordinates
disable the same rival in different stages.

\section{Finding certificates}\label{sec:mine}

Checking a certificate is a proof; finding one is a search, and the two
should not be confused. We keep them in separate programs, with the
checker as the only authority. The miner may fail, and it may propose
nonsense; nothing it proposes counts until the checker accepts it, and
the checker's verdict is symbolic in the phase index rather than a range
check. Consequently the miner needs no correctness argument at all --- a
liberating architecture, and the same one used by SAT-based verification
and by Coq-BB5.

The search itself is short to describe. Simulate. For each rule, take the
positions that begin a maximal run of it; these are boundary candidates,
because a rigid machine's phases begin the same way every time. Fit each
coordinate of the candidate boundary states to $\mathrm{EXP}$ exactly over
$\mathbb{Q}$ --- three unknowns, solved on three phases and required to
reproduce all the others. Segment each phase word into runs and blocks by
looking for maximal repetitions of a short sub-word. Require the resulting
\emph{stage signature} to be identical across phases of the same parity;
this is precisely the rigidity test, and it is where digit-consuming
machines fail. Fit the repetition counts. Finally --- an economy worth
noting --- do \emph{not} fit the block start states: obtain them by
symbolically composing the earlier stages, exactly as the checker will.

\section{Where the method stops}\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Thin margins: one step past the boundary}

Every machine we decide survives by a comfortable margin: a coordinate
that grows linearly in the phase index, or an invariant preserved
exactly. Contrast the one previously decided $\mathrm{BB}(6)$ machine of
comparable shape, whose halting reduces to the $2$-adic valuation of
$2\cdot 3^{i} + i + 5$ staying above a linear bound~\cite{bwmachine}.
That orbit is rigid --- there is a closed form --- but its margin is
\emph{thin}: it is a near-coincidence between powers of $2$ and powers of
$3$, and controlling it needs Baker's theorem on linear forms in
logarithms~\cite{bakerwustholz}. The published proof accordingly invokes
Baker--W\"ustholz as an axiom.

So rigidity is necessary but not sufficient: what also matters is whether
the surviving quantity has room. Our nine, and the sixteen new ones, have
room; that is why nothing beyond guard arithmetic and induction appears
anywhere in this paper. It is a useful triage question for any new
machine: is the orbit closed-form, and if so, is the margin thick or
thin?

\subsection{Symmetry is exhausted: a negative result}

A natural hope for shrinking a holdout list is symmetry: if two programs
differ only by renaming primes, deciding one decides the other. There is
a catch that turns out to be decisive. Every program starts at $n = 2$,
so a relabelling $\pi$ transports the \emph{start} to $e_{\pi(2)}$; the
halting-from-$2$ question transfers only when $\pi$ fixes the prime $2$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:symm}
Under the relabellings that fix the prime $2$, the official $21{,}233$
machine list has $21{,}233$ distinct classes: \textbf{no two machines on
the list are related by a sound relabelling}. Under all prime
relabellings --- which do \emph{not} transfer the start --- the list
collapses to $15{,}600$ classes, so $5{,}633$ machines ($26.5\%$) are
dynamically conjugate to another entry while remaining genuinely distinct
questions. \Meas
\end{proposition}

The list is thus already canonical with respect to the only symmetry that
helps, and the remaining $26.5\%$ is a mirage: those pairs relate
``machine $A$ from $2$'' to ``machine $B$ from some other prime''. The
practical residue is smaller but real --- conjugate machines have
template-isomorphic certificates, so the expensive part, the phase
analysis, transfers even though the decision does not. We record the
negative result because it is the kind of thing worth knowing before
spending a week on it.

\subsection{The wall}

What is left is digit-consuming, and it is most of everything:
$675$ of the $694$ refined holdouts. For those, no boundary family in
$\mathrm{EXP}$ --- indeed none with a bounded description --- tracks the
orbit, so the certificate class is empty for them by construction rather
than by budget. This is the same wall that stands in front of Collatz.
We make no claim to address it, and we would be suspicious of a method
that claimed to without saying which of the two behaviours above it had
overcome.

\section{The Lean development}\label{sec:lean}

The formalization uses Lean~4 with \textbf{no} \texttt{mathlib}: core
arithmetic, \texttt{omega}, and kernel \texttt{decide}. It contains no
\texttt{sorry}, no \texttt{admit}, no \texttt{native\_decide} and no
\texttt{axiom} declarations; every non-halting theorem depends on exactly
Lean's three core axioms, and the entry lemmas on none.

Two parts are worth distinguishing. \texttt{Decider.lean} formalizes the
\emph{soundness argument} --- Theorems~\ref{thm:A} and~\ref{thm:B} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:unsound} --- with four machines re-derived through
the abstract theorem to confirm the contract is what the concrete proofs
establish. The per-machine files formalize nine machines individually. We
do \emph{not} claim the Python checker is verified: what is proved is
that no certificate meeting the contract can exist for a halting machine.
The sixteen new decisions are \Chk{}, not \Lean{}; transcribing them is
mechanical and is the natural next step, most efficiently done by
formalizing the checker itself so that each machine reduces to evaluating
a decidable predicate on certificate data.

\section{Reproducibility and provenance}\label{sec:repro}

Code, certificates, logs and the Lean development accompany the paper.
The checker runs the certificates in about a second; a falsifier that
perturbs boundaries, counts, rule indices and block coefficients produced
$540$ corrupted certificates, all rejected. Holdout lists are those
published by the BBFractran project~\cite{bbfractran}; machine reductions
quoted from the bbchallenge wiki~\cite{bbchallenge} appear verbatim beside
the code that uses them.
